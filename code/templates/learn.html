How ravenSDR works

From a radio wave to a neural network, one step at a time.
← back to the console

1 A radio wave is a spinning arrow

Everything else follows from this one picture.

Imagine spinning a jump rope. There are only three things you can change about it, and every radio mode in the world is a way of wiggling one of them:

  • how big the swing is — amplitude
  • how fast it spins — frequency
  • where in the circle it is right now — phase
The spinning arrow, and the two numbers your dongle records
I — how far across Q — how far up the arrow itself
Your SDR does not record "radio". It writes down the arrow's position — two numbers — 2,400,000 times a second.

That is all IQ means. I is how far across the arrow points, Q is how far up. Two numbers, and from them you can recover everything: the length of the arrow is the strength, and how fast the angle turns is the frequency.

Why two numbers and not one? Because with only "how far across", an arrow spinning clockwise looks identical to one spinning anticlockwise. You could not tell a station above your dial from one below it. The second number breaks the tie.

Why AFSK is the odd one. The radio has no idea it is carrying data — it thinks somebody is talking. That is why decoding APRS takes two stages: rtl_fm first undoes the FM to recover the "audio", and only then does multimon-ng listen to the beeps. It is a modem shouting down a voice radio.

3 Turning radio into a picture

The trick that lets us use image AI on radio.

A list of two million number-pairs per second means nothing to a human, or to a neural network. So we ask a different question, over and over: how much energy is there at each frequency, right now? That is what an FFT does.

Stack those answers over time and you get a spectrogram — a picture. Time runs down, frequency across, brightness is power.

Building a spectrogram, one FFT slice at a time
Each row is one FFT. The picture builds downward as time passes — exactly what your node does before every classification.

This is the pivot: we have turned a radio problem into an image problem, and computers are extremely good at images.

4 Teaching it to recognise them

What "training" actually is.

Training is flashcards. Show the network a picture, let it guess, tell it the right answer, nudge it slightly towards being right. Repeat a few hundred thousand times.

One training step
The "answer on the back of the card" is the label — and where it comes from decides everything.

Normally a human labels thousands of examples by hand. Your node gets them free: when it tunes to 94.9 MHz it knows that is WFM, so every sample collected there is labelled automatically.

Learning curve — and the trap underneath it
accuracy on cards it has seen accuracy on cards it has never seen

5 Where it goes wrong

Every problem we hit was a data problem, not a maths problem.
The classroom trap
Imagine every maths lesson happened in room A and every history lesson in room B. A student could score 100% by learning the rooms — and would be lost the moment you moved them.
This happened to us. Every modulation was collected from exactly one frequency — WFM only from 94.9, ACARS only from 131.55. The model scored 99.1% and may have learned nothing but which band it was looking at. Fixed by collecting FM from 17 different frequencies, so the only thing those captures share is the modulation itself.
Class balance — live from this node
Loading live corpus…
Why balance matters. If 40% of the flashcards say "WFM" and 2% say "APRS", the network learns it can score well by guessing WFM constantly. It would be right often and useless always.

And the labels have to be true. We were recording static from an antenna that cannot hear 8 MHz and filing it under "WEFAX" — which would teach the model that weather-fax looks like noise. Worse than having no examples at all.

6 Onto the chip

Why training happens elsewhere, and inference happens here.
The whole pipeline
Hover a stage. The Hailo NPU only ever runs the last one — it is an inference accelerator and cannot train.

The trained model is PyTorch, which the NPU cannot read. So it is exported to ONNX (a portable format), then the Hailo compiler turns it into a .hef file. That step also quantises it — swapping precise decimals for small integers, which is what makes the chip fast and power-thrifty.

Why not train on the Pi? The Hailo compiler only runs on x86, and the Pi is ARM. So the corpus is collected here, shipped to a VM to train and compile, and only the finished .hef comes back.

Four libraries do the work. Nothing here is bespoke maths — it is mostly gluing well-worn tools together.

Why torch is not on the Pi. Training needs gradients, optimisers and float32 — hundreds of megabytes of machinery used once. The Pi only ever runs a finished model, so it needs none of it. PyTorch was deliberately removed from the node.

Step 1 — IQ becomes a picture

Slide a window along the samples, FFT each one, stack the results. Roughly twenty lines, in signal_classifier.py:

def iq_to_spectrogram(iq_samples, fft_size=256, hop=128):
    window = np.hanning(fft_size)          # taper the edges of each slice
    n_frames = (len(iq_samples) - fft_size) // hop + 1
    spectrogram = np.zeros((n_frames, fft_size))

    for i in range(n_frames):
        frame = iq_samples[i*hop : i*hop + fft_size]
        fft   = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft(frame * window))
        power = np.maximum(np.abs(fft) ** 2, 1e-20)   # never log(0)
        spectrogram[i] = 10 * np.log10(power)         # into dB

    return spectrogram                     # (frames, 256) — one row per slice

hanning tapers each slice to zero at its edges. Without it, chopping the signal creates artificial sharp edges, and an FFT reports those as energy that was never on the air. fftshift just moves zero Hz to the middle so negative frequencies sit left of centre where you would expect them.

Then squash to a greyscale image the network can eat:

def spectrogram_to_image(spec, size=224):
    lo, hi = spec.min(), spec.max()
    img = ((spec - lo) / (hi - lo) * 255).astype(np.uint8)   # scale to 0-255
    rows = (np.arange(size) * img.shape[0] / size).astype(int)
    cols = (np.arange(size) * img.shape[1] / size).astype(int)
    return img[np.ix_(rows, cols)]         # nearest-neighbour resize
A trap hiding in that first line. Every image is scaled by its own min and max, so absolute signal strength is thrown away — a strong signal and a weak one look identical. That is deliberate (the model should not care how close you are), but it means a window of pure noise gets stretched to full contrast and can look like a real signal. It is exactly why a separate is there actually anything here check was needed before collecting.

Step 2 — borrowing a network that can already see

We do not build a network from scratch. MobileNetV2 has already been trained on a million photographs, so it knows edges, textures and stripes. We keep all of that and replace only the final layer — the part that says which of 1000 everyday objects it is — with one that picks between our modulations:

model = torchvision.models.mobilenet_v2(weights=IMAGENET1K_V1)
model.classifier[1] = nn.Linear(1280, num_classes)   # 1000 objects -> our classes

That is transfer learning, and it is why a few thousand samples suffice where training from nothing would need millions.

What gets kept and what gets replaced

The two halves even learn at different speeds — nudge the borrowed layers gently, teach the new one properly:

optimizer = optim.Adam([
    {"params": model.features.parameters(),   "lr": 1e-5},   # already knows how to see
    {"params": model.classifier.parameters(), "lr": 1e-3},   # knows nothing yet
])

Step 3 — the training loop

This is the whole of "machine learning". Five lines, repeated:

for images, labels in loader:          # a batch of ~32 spectrograms
    optimizer.zero_grad()              # forget last batch's corrections
    outputs = model(images)            # guess
    loss    = criterion(outputs, labels)   # how wrong, as one number
    loss.backward()                    # work out which weights caused it
    optimizer.step()                   # nudge every weight slightly

loss.backward() is the clever part: it walks the network in reverse working out how much each of the 2.2 million weights contributed to the error. optimizer.step() then moves each one a little in the direction that would have been less wrong. Do that tens of thousands of times and the network has learned.

Gradient descent — rolling downhill to the lowest error
The "learning rate" is how far it moves each step. Too big and it bounces straight over the bottom.
Why the loss is weighted here. Our corpus is lopsided, so CrossEntropyLoss(weight=...) makes a mistake on a rare class cost more than one on a common class. Without it, guessing "WFM" every time is a decent strategy, and the network will find it.

Step 4 — out of Python, onto the chip

# PyTorch -> ONNX: a portable description of the network
torch.onnx.export(model, torch.randn(1, 3, 224, 224), "signal_classifier.onnx")

# ONNX -> HEF: Hailo's compiler, x86 only
runner = ClientRunner(hw_arch="hailo8l")
runner.translate_onnx_model(onnx_path, start_node_names=["input"])
runner.optimize(calibration_images)    # measure typical values, pick integer scales
hef = runner.compile()

optimize() is the quantisation step. The network thinks in decimals; the NPU works in 8-bit integers. To convert, the compiler needs to see ~100 real images to learn what range of values to expect — then it can map decimals onto integers without losing the plot. That is why calibration data has to look like what the model will really see.

And on the Pi, running it is four calls:

configured = model.configure()
bindings   = configured.create_bindings()
bindings.input().set_buffer(image)      # NHWC float32, 0..1
configured.run([bindings], timeout_ms)
logits = bindings.output().get_buffer()
probabilities = softmax(logits)         # the graph ends at a Linear layer

8 Inside the network

What is actually happening when a picture goes in and a label comes out.

A neural network is a stack of very simple things. One neuron does exactly this and nothing more:

output = activation( w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + … + wₙ·xₙ + bias )

Multiply each input by a weight, add them up, add a bias, then pass the result through an activation function. That is the whole unit. "Learning" means finding good values for the w's and b's — MobileNetV2 has about 2.2 million of them.

Feed-forward — signal flowing left to right
Line brightness is the weight. Watch a value enter on the left, get multiplied and summed at every node, and emerge as a decision.
Why the activation function matters more than it looks. Without it, stacking layers is pointless: a linear function of a linear function is still just a linear function, so a hundred layers would collapse into the power of one. The non-linearity is what lets depth buy you anything. Modern networks mostly use ReLU — max(0, x) — because it is trivially cheap and does not saturate the way older S-shaped functions did.
Activation functions

How it learns: backpropagation

The forward pass produces a guess. The loss turns "how wrong" into a single number. Then the chain rule from calculus is applied backwards through the network to work out, for every one of those 2.2 million weights, how much this particular weight contributed to the error. That quantity is the gradient.

Each weight then moves a small step in the direction that reduces the error:

w  ←  w  −  learning_rate × ∂loss/∂w

That single line is gradient descent. Everything else — Adam, momentum, schedules — is refinement of how big a step and in what direction.

The interview question hiding here: why not just solve for the best weights directly? Because the loss surface over 2.2 million dimensions has no closed form and is wildly non-convex. Gradient descent is not elegant, it is simply the only thing that works at this scale.

9 Why a convolutional network

The idea that makes image recognition tractable at all.

Our spectrogram is 224×224. If the first layer were fully connected — every pixel wired to every neuron — one layer of 1000 neurons would need 50 million weights. For one layer. It would also be brittle: move the signal 3 pixels left and every weight is wrong.

A convolution instead slides one small kernel (say 3×3) across the whole image, reusing the same nine weights everywhere:

A 3×3 kernel sliding across the image
Nine weights, reused across every position. That is weight sharing, and it is why CNNs are small enough to train.

Two properties fall out of this, and both matter for radio:

  • Translation invariance — a burst is recognised wherever it appears in the window, not only where it appeared in training.
  • Parameter efficiency — 9 weights instead of 50 million.

Stack these and the network builds a hierarchy on its own: early layers find edges, middle layers find textures and stripes, later layers find whole structures. That is exactly what we borrow from ImageNet — the early layers of a photo network are just as useful on a spectrogram, because edges are edges.

What one InvertedResidual actually does

17 of the 19 blocks are this one pattern, so it is worth knowing cold. It is the opposite shape to a classic ResNet block — hence "inverted":

Expand → filter cheaply → squeeze
The expensive 3×3 never touches the wide tensor — it only ever runs one filter per channel. That is the whole trick.
  1. Expand — a 1×1 convolution multiplies channels by 6 (e.g. 24 → 144). Cheap, because 1×1 does no spatial work.
  2. Depthwise 3×3 — one small filter per channel, independently. This is where spatial pattern-finding happens, and it costs 144×9 instead of 144×144×9.
  3. Project — a 1×1 squeezes back down (144 → 24). No activation here — a ReLU on a narrow layer destroys information, which is the "linear bottleneck" in the paper's title.
  4. Skip connection — if input and output shapes match, add the input back. Gradients then have a short path back to early layers, which is what makes deep networks trainable at all.
Reading the table above: blocks marked +skip in our dump are the ones where that addition happens — they keep the same shape in and out. Blocks that change shape (2, 4, 7, 11, 14, 17) cannot, so they do the downsampling.
Why this matters for the Hailo. Depthwise convolutions and 1×1 projections quantise to int8 well and are natively supported by the compiler. Exotic architectures often will not compile at all — MobileNetV2 was chosen partly because it is in the Hailo Model Zoo and known to produce a working .hef for HAILO8L.

Why MobileNetV2 specifically

The choice was made for the NPU, and it is worth being able to defend it.

ReasonDetail
Depthwise separable convolutions Splits one convolution into a per-channel filter plus a 1×1 mix — roughly 8–9× less computation for nearly the same accuracy.
Inverted residuals Expand to a wide layer, filter cheaply, squeeze back down. Keeps the expensive part narrow.
~3.4M parameters ResNet-50 is 25M. Ours has to fit and run on a 13 TOPS edge chip alongside Whisper.
In the Hailo Model Zoo Known to compile to .hef for HAILO8L. An architecture that will not compile is worthless here however accurate it is.
Standard vs depthwise separable convolution
Same job, a fraction of the multiplications — which is the entire reason this runs on a Pi instead of a workstation.

10 Explaining this in an interview

The decisions, the trade-offs, and the failures worth admitting.

The engineering here is not the model — it is everything around the model. Anyone can fine-tune MobileNetV2. What is worth talking about is the data and the constraints.

Decisions you should be able to defend

DecisionWhyWhat it cost
Spectrograms, not raw IQ Turns a signal problem into an image problem, so a pre-trained vision network can be reused. Throws away absolute phase; the FFT window size fixes a time/frequency trade-off.
Transfer learning A few thousand samples is enough; from scratch would need millions. Inherits ImageNet's biases and a fixed 224×224 input.
Preset as ground truth Free labels — tuned to 94.9 MHz means the sample is WFM. Only true if the antenna can hear that band, and only during a burst on bursty protocols.
int8 quantisation Required by the NPU; makes it fast and low-power. Small accuracy loss, and calibration data must match production data.
Train off-node Hailo's compiler is x86-only; the Pi is ARM. A deploy pipeline instead of a single machine.

The failures — these are the good part

An interviewer learns far more from how you found a bug than from a headline number. Every one of these was a data problem wearing a modelling costume:

SymptomActual causeHow it surfaced
99.1% test accuracy Each class came from exactly one frequency, so the model could learn the band and never the modulation. Held whole frequencies out of training; FM recall fell to 0.38.
1921 "OOK" samples Empty channel. 0% contained a burst, only 2× the noise floor. Compared spectral peak-to-median against a known-noise reference.
Strong "APRS" samples A steady carrier parked on 144.390 — not packets at all. High spectral energy but 3% burst rate: contradictory for a packet mode.
Half the corpus vanished Preset modulations were never in the class list; the loader dropped them silently. Sample count did not match what was on disk.
The line worth having ready: "A random train/test split cannot detect a confound where each class only appears in one context. I split by frequency instead — held entire bands out of training — and accuracy dropped from 99% to 38%, which told me the first number was measuring the wrong thing."

Running it without the NPU

  
Compiling for the Hailo needs its Dataflow Compiler — x86-only and behind a
  login. Rather than wait, the same trained network runs on the Pi's CPU through
  onnxruntime. Identical predictions, just slower. The backend order is:


Hailo NPU        →  a few ms      (needs a compiled .hef)
trained ONNX CPU →  57.8 ms       ← running now
heuristics       →  instant, and can only ever say WFM/FM/CW/AM/SSB


  
One subtlety that fails silently if you get it wrong: torch.onnx.export
  records NCHW, while HailoRT expects NHWC. Same model,
  different memory convention. Feed the wrong one and you get confident nonsense rather
  than an error, so each backend builds its own tensor.
